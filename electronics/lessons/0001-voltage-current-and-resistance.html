Voltage, Current, and Resistance

Three quantities, one equation. Everything else in this course is built on top of them — including the reason your robot's motor will happily destroy itself if you let it.
By the end of this lesson You will be able to look at a simple circuit, and predict how much current will flow through it before you ever connect it — in your head, in seconds. That single skill is what separates building circuits from guessing at them.

Where this is going

Your robot is, electrically, a very simple thing: a battery, two motors, a couple of sensors, and a chip deciding what to do. Every problem you will hit while building it — a motor that won't turn, a sensor that reads nonsense, a chip that resets whenever the wheels start, a part that gets too hot to touch — is a problem about how much current is flowing where.

So we start there. Not because it's traditional, but because you cannot debug what you cannot predict.

The three quantities

Electricity is charge moving through a conductor. Three numbers describe that, and it's worth being fussy about the difference between them, because beginners mix up the first two constantly.

QuantitySymbolUnitWhat it actually is
VoltageVvolt (V) The difference in electrical “pressure” between two points. It is always between two points — asking “the voltage here” is incomplete, like asking for an altitude with no sea level.
CurrentIampere (A) The rate at which charge flows past a point. One amp is a lot in hobby electronics; most things you'll meet are milliamps (mA).
ResistanceRohm (Ω) How much a material opposes that flow. A property of the component, not of the circuit it's in.

The standard analogy is water in a pipe, and it's a good one as long as you don't lean on it too hard:

  • Voltage is the pressure difference pushing water along the pipe.
  • Current is the flow rate — litres per second past a point.
  • Resistance is the narrowness of the pipe, restricting that flow.

The most useful consequence, and the one people find least intuitive: a battery does not supply a fixed current. It supplies a fixed voltage. How much current flows is decided by what you connect across it. A 9 V battery with nothing attached delivers zero amps. The same battery with a paperclip across it delivers as much as it physically can, and gets dangerously hot. The circuit decides the current.

An analogy from your day job

Voltage is the pressure your system is under; current is the actual request rate flowing through; resistance is how much the bottleneck throttles it. You don't get to set throughput directly — you set the pressure and the size of the pipe, and throughput falls out of both. Same here, and unlike in software, the equation is exact.

Ohm's law

Those three quantities are related by one equation, and it is not an approximation or a rule of thumb — for ordinary resistive components it is simply how the universe behaves:

I = V / R   amps = volts ÷ ohms

Which rearranges the two other ways you'll need constantly:

V = I × R      R = V / I

Read the first one aloud as a sentence and it stops being algebra: the current that flows is the pressure you apply, divided by the opposition it meets. Push harder, more flows. Restrict it more, less flows.

One unit trap that will bite you: currents are usually quoted in milliamps, but the equation wants amps. 20 mA is 0.02 A. Getting this wrong by a factor of 1000 is the single most common beginner arithmetic error.

See it move

Here is the entire circuit: a battery, and a resistor across it. It starts at 6 V — the pack your robot will likely run on. Drag the sliders. The dots are charge flowing: watch how fast they move, and notice they move at the same speed everywhere in the loop, because current in a single loop is the same everywhere in it.

Three things to actually try, rather than just wiggling the sliders:

  • Halve the resistance. The current exactly doubles. That proportionality is Ohm's law — you're looking at it.
  • Set the resistance to its minimum, voltage to maximum. Over two amps, and several watts of heat in a part designed to dissipate a quarter of one. This is what "short circuit" means, and it's not an abstract hazard: it's the state your motor is briefly in every time it starts.
  • Watch the dot speed, not the dot count. Current is a rate. More current is the same charges moving faster past you.

What you now know

You can take any circuit consisting of a source and a resistance, and state the current in it without building it. That is genuinely the foundation — the transistor lesson, the motor-driver lesson and the battery-life lesson are all this same equation wearing different clothes.

Two things were deliberately left hanging, and they're the next two lessons:

  • Real circuits have more than one component. When several parts share a loop, how do the voltage and the current divide between them? That's series and parallel, and it's what lets you build a voltage divider — the circuit almost every sensor you'll use is secretly built from.
  • Not everything obeys Ohm's law. An LED doesn't. A motor doesn't, quite. A transistor emphatically doesn't — and that disobedience is exactly what makes it useful. Understanding where Ohm's law stops applying is what the semiconductor lessons are for.
