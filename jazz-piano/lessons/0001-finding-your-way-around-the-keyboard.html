Finding Your Way Around the Keyboard

One pattern that lets you name any note on the piano — without an instrument, without counting from the edge, and without reading a note of music.
Why this, first? You're starting from zero, and the goal is to eventually sit down and play jazz standards for yourself. Every chord, every voicing, every shape you'll ever learn on this keyboard is found by pattern, not by counting letters from the far left of the piano. Today's win is small but load-bearing: you'll be able to point to any natural note by sight, instantly. That's the map everything else gets drawn on. (See your mission.)

The idea, in 90 seconds

Look at the black keys on a piano and you'll notice they don't repeat evenly — they come in a repeating pattern of a group of two, then a group of three, all the way up and down the keyboard.

"Find a group of two black keys. The white key just to their left is always C. Every time."

That's the entire trick for this lesson. The seven white keys are the "natural" notes — C, D, E, F, G, A, B — and then the alphabet just resets back to C. Once you can spot C by its neighboring black-key group, you can name any white key just by counting forward from C: D is next, then E, then F, and so on.

The one wrinkle worth knowing: two white-key pairs have no black key between them — E→F and B→C. That gap is exactly what creates the 2-then-3 grouping in the first place. There's a full breakdown in the Keyboard Map reference.

One of those C's, near the middle of a full-size piano, gets a special name: middle C. It's just a shared reference point musicians use — you'll find it below by the same trick, no different from any other C.
