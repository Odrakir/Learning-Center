Minor 7th and Dominant 7th Chords

Same root, same fixed 5th — only two notes ever move. Learn what moves, and you know all three jazz 7th chords.
Why this, now? You already have one jazz 7th chord — maj7 (Lesson 2). Today you get the other two: minor 7th (min7) and dominant 7th (dom7). Together, these three colors are almost everything you'll see on a lead sheet. They're also exactly the three chords in jazz's most common progression — the ii–V–I — which is next. (See your mission.)

The idea, in 90 seconds

Every 7th chord has a root and a 5th that never move — the 5th is always 7 half steps above the root, no matter the chord's color. What changes the flavor is just two notes: the 3rd and the 7th. Each can be "major" (higher) or "flatted" (one half step lower).

maj7root · +4 · +3 · +4
↓ flat the 7th (last step: 4 → 3)
dom7 ("7")root · +4 · +3 · +3
↓ also flat the 3rd (first step: 4 → 3)
min7root · +3 · +4 · +3

"Only two notes ever move — the 3rd and the 7th. Flatten the 7th and bright maj7 turns bluesy dom7. Flatten the 3rd too, and it turns dark, mellow min7."

That's genuinely the whole lesson. Dominant 7 keeps maj7's bright major 3rd but flattens the 7th — it sounds unstable, like it wants to resolve somewhere (you'll feel exactly why in the ii–V–I lesson). Minor 7 flattens both the 3rd and the 7th — mellower, darker, more "at rest" than dominant.

On a lead sheet these get written short: maj7 stays Cmaj7; minor 7th is usually just Cm7; and dominant 7th drops the word entirely — plain C7 always means dominant. You'll use this shorthand once you start reading real charts.

Check yourself

Aim to get all six right, missing a chord is fine — the widget reveals the correct spelling and you study it before moving on. What you're really practicing is the branching logic: flat the 7th first, then decide whether the 3rd is flat too. That decision, made fast, is what lets you read Dm7 or G7 off a page and know instantly what to play.
