Reading a Lead Sheet

The one page a jazz musician actually plays from — melody on top, chord symbols above. Today you read a real one.
Why this, now? You can build all three 7th-chord colors and string them into a ii–V–I. But jazz isn't played from note-for-note sheet music — it's played from a lead sheet: just the tune and the chord symbols, leaving the rest to you. Learning to read one is the bridge between "I know some chords" and "I can sit down and play a standard." Today you'll read the opening of a real one — Fly Me to the Moon — and find out you can already play most of it. (See your mission.)

What a lead sheet is

A lead sheet strips a song down to the two things a jazz player needs and no more: the melody (written on the staff) and the chord symbols (the letters above it). It does not tell you exactly which notes to play in your left hand, or the rhythm to play them in — that's your job, and it's why ten pianists play the same lead sheet ten different ways.

Here's the anatomy. Each label points to one thing you need to recognize on sight:

Staff — the 5 lines the melody sits on Measure — one "bar," here holding one chord Chord symbol — a shape you already know

"Melody on the staff, chords above it, bars in between. That's the whole document — everything else you supply yourself."

Each bar names a chord you already know how to build.

Look at what you're reading: Am7 · Dm7 · G7 · Cmaj7. Bars 2–4 are Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7 — the exact ii–V–I in C you drilled last lesson. The Am7 in front of it is the vi chord (root a whole step below the tonic C — or, the same note as the 6th degree). You already know every chord in the opening of a jazz standard. That's the whole point of learning the shapes first.

Reading the melody line

The chord letters are easy — you've been reading those since Lesson 3. The genuinely new skill is reading a note on the staff and finding it on the keyboard. You don't need full sight-reading for this mission; you need to recognize a handful of landmark notes and count from them.

Start from middle C — it hangs one short "ledger" line just below the staff. From there, every step up the alphabet moves up one position: onto the bottom line (E), the space above it (F), and so on, alternating line, space, line, space:

Ledger line — a stubby extra line for notes that fall below (or above) the staff, like middle C.

Check yourself

Reading a lead sheet is really two habits stacked: glance at the chord symbol and know the shape (that's review), and glance at a staff note and know the key (that's today). When both feel automatic on this four-bar phrase, you're reading — and you're one step from playing a whole tune. A full standard is just more of these bars in a row, most of them chords you already own.
