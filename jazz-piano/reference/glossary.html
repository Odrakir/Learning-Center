Jazz Piano — Glossary

The shared vocabulary for this workspace. Terms here are used consistently across every lesson. Grows lesson by lesson.

Keyboard geography

Octave
The span from one note to the next note of the same letter name (e.g. C to the next C). On a full 88-key piano there are just over 7 of them. Same letter, same "color" of sound, different pitch height.
Middle C
The C nearest the middle of a full-size keyboard — the anchor pitch musicians use as a shared reference point. Found via the black-key pattern, not by counting from the edge of the keyboard.
Natural note
Any of the seven white-key letter names: A, B, C, D, E, F, G. The alphabet resets after G back to A.
Sharp (♯) / flat (♭)
A black key, named relative to the white key next to it. The same black key is both the sharp of the note below it and the flat of the note above it (e.g. C♯ = D♭) — which name is used depends on context, covered in a later lesson.
Half step (semitone)
The smallest distance between two notes on a piano — one key to the very next key, black or white, with nothing in between.
Whole step (tone)
Two half steps. Most, but not all, neighboring white keys are a whole step apart (E–F and B–C are the exceptions — only a half step, no black key between them).

Chords (introduced from Lesson 2 onward)

Interval
The distance between two notes, measured in steps. Chords are built by stacking specific intervals on top of a root note.
7th chord
A four-note chord — the basic building block of jazz harmony (as opposed to the three-note "triads" of most beginner piano method books). The three core colors: major 7th, minor 7th, and dominant 7th.
Root
The note a chord is built up from — the note that gives the chord its letter name. Every other chord tone is measured in half steps above the root.
Chord tone
Any one of the notes in a chord, referred to by its position above the root: the 3rd, the 5th, the 7th, and so on.
Major 7th chord (maj7)
A 7th chord built root, +4 half steps (major 3rd), +3 more (to the 5th), +4 more (to the major 7th) — e.g. Cmaj7 = C E G B. Bright, lush "home" sound. Written Cmaj7 on a lead sheet. Full pattern and worked examples in the 7th Chord Shapes reference.
Dominant 7th chord (dom7 / "7")
A 7th chord built root, +4 (major 3rd), +3 more (5th), +3 more (flatted 7th) — e.g. C7 = C E G B♭. Bluesy, unstable — wants to resolve. Only the 7th is flattened relative to maj7. Written as a bare C7 on a lead sheet — no extra word means dominant. Introduced in Lesson 3.
Minor 7th chord (min7)
A 7th chord built root, +3 (minor 3rd), +4 more (5th), +3 more (flatted 7th) — e.g. Cm7 = C E♭ G B♭. Dark, mellow — both the 3rd and 7th are flattened relative to maj7. Written Cm7 on a lead sheet. Introduced in Lesson 3.
Voicing
A specific way of arranging a chord's notes on the keyboard (which notes, which octave, which hand). The same chord can be voiced many ways; jazz players choose voicings for sound and hand comfort, not just theoretical correctness.

Progressions (introduced from Lesson 4 onward)

Tonic
The "home" note of a key — the note everything else is measured against and wants to resolve back to. Labeled roman numeral I.
Roman numeral (chord function)
A label for a chord based on its root's position above the tonic, not its letter name — so the same numeral means the same role in any key. Uppercase (I, V) means a major or dominant chord; lowercase (ii) means minor.
ii–V–I
Jazz's most common chord progression: a minor 7th (ii, root a whole step / +2 half steps above the tonic) moving to a dominant 7th (V, root a 5th / +7 half steps above the tonic) moving to the tonic major 7th (I). In the key of C: Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7. Full walkthrough in Lesson 4.
Circle of fourths
The pattern of chord roots moving up a 4th (or down a 5th) each time — the D→G→C motion in a ii–V–I is one bite of it. The engine behind most jazz root movement; covered in depth in a later lesson.
